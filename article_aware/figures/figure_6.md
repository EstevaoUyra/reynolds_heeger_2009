# Figure 6 - Feature-Based Attention and Tuning Sharpening

## Role in the paper

Figure 6 demonstrates the model's account of feature-based attention in MT: when spatial
attention remains away from the recorded neuron's receptive field but the attended
opposite-hemifield stimulus shares the motion direction of the RF stimulus, the attention
field multiplies stimulus drive selectively in motion-direction space. The result is
nonmultiplicative tuning-curve sharpening rather than the pure multiplicative scaling shown
for broad-feature spatial attention in Figure 5. The key model claim is that selective
feature gain narrows the response tuning curve because the stimulus drive is multiplied by
an attention field that is itself tuned for motion direction (C-017, C-023).

## Verbatim caption

> "Feature-Based Attention Can Cause a Sharpening of Tuning Curves. (A) Stimulus and task. A pair of stimuli were presented simultaneously while recording responses of a neuron in visual cortical area MT. One stimulus was in the receptive field of the recorded neuron and the other was in the opposite hemifield. The directions of the two stimuli were yoked. The monkey was cued to attend either to the fixation point (dashed blue circle), or to the stimulus in the opposite hemifield (dashed red circle) to detect a change in speed or direction. That is, spatial attention was always directed away from the receptive field, but feature-based attention was matched to the stimulus in the receptive field on half the trials. (B) Feature-based attention caused a sharpening of motion direction tuning (adapted from Martinez-Trujillo and Treue, 2004). Blue, responses when attention was directed to the fixation point. Red, responses when attention was directed to the stimulus in the opposite hemifield. (C) Model simulations yielded similar results. Blue, responses of a model neuron when the attention field was flat (equal) for all motion directions, and spatial attention was directed away from the model neuron's receptive field. Red, responses when attention was again directed away from the simulated neuron's receptive field but to the same direction of motion as the stimulus in the receptive field."

## Pipeline and expected behavior

### Panel A - Martinez-Trujillo and Treue task schematic

Panel A shows the empirical protocol that motivates the model simulation. Two moving
stimuli are presented simultaneously: one falls in the recorded MT neuron's receptive field
and the other lies in the opposite hemifield. The two stimuli always move in yoked
directions, so changing the stimulus direction changes both stimuli together. The two task
conditions direct spatial attention away from the recorded RF in both cases: either to
fixation or to the opposite-hemifield stimulus (C-017). Because the Figure 6 implementation
protocol only generates the panel C model tuning curves, panel A should be treated as
context unless a renderer chooses to draw a coarse schematic.

### Panel B - Empirical feature-based sharpening

Panel B is the empirical reference result. It shows motion-direction tuning measured in MT,
with responses plotted for attention to fixation and attention to the opposite-hemifield
stimulus. The caption states that feature-based attention caused sharpening: the
opposite-hemifield-stimulus attention condition produces a narrower tuning curve than the
fixation-attention condition (C-017, C-023). Because panel B is adapted empirical data, the
implementation should not be judged on point locations, error bars, or exact firing-rate
values from this panel unless an explicit data-extraction protocol is added.

### Panel C - Model simulation of feature-based sharpening

Panel C is generated by the standard attention-normalization pipeline. For each swept
motion direction, the protocol builds stimulus drive E as the sum of two equal-contrast
stimuli with the same direction: one at the recorded RF center and one in the opposite
hemifield (EQ-stim, A-009). It then constructs attention field A in one of two ways. In the
attend-fixation condition, spatial attention is away from the recorded RF and the feature
dimension is flat, so the RF neuron's response is effectively the baseline tuning response
under spatial attention away from the RF (C-017). In the attend-opposite-hemifield-stimulus
condition, the attention field is centered away from the RF but is selective for the current
motion direction with tuning width 60 degrees and peak gain gamma = 2 (C-017).

The response is computed with the attention-modulated drive in both numerator and
suppressive pool: S is the convolution of A times E with the suppressive kernel, and R is
A times E divided by S plus sigma, followed by rectification (EQ-5, EQ-6, C-005, C-006).
Because the attended opposite-hemifield stimulus has the same direction as the RF stimulus,
feature-based gain is matched to the RF stimulus direction on each trial. Near the recorded
neuron's preferred direction, the feature-selective attention field reinforces the same
directional channel as the stimulus drive. Away from the preferred direction, the product of
the neuron's own stimulus-drive tuning and the feature-selective attention field falls off
more quickly. This multiplication of two direction-selective profiles is the model reason
the attend-opposite-stimulus curve is narrower than the attend-fixation curve (EQ-stim,
EQ-attention, C-017, C-023).

The expected panel C output is therefore a two-curve normalized tuning plot. Both curves
peak near theta_stim = 0, both fall toward the direction extremes, and the
opposite-hemifield-stimulus attention curve has a smaller full width at half maximum than
the attend-fixation curve. The expected behavior in the pseudocode states this quantitatively
as a smaller FWHM for `attend_opposite_stimulus_tuning` than for
`attend_fixation_tuning` (C-023).

## Key inter-panel relationships

1. **Panel C is the model reproduction target:** Panels A and B explain the experimental
   setup and empirical target, but the implementation protocol generates only the model
   tuning curves for Figure 6C. Visual review should therefore focus on panel C and should
   not require pointwise reproduction of panel B empirical data (C-017).

2. **Same spatial-away condition, different feature selectivity:** In both panel B
   conditions and in both panel C model conditions, spatial attention is away from the RF.
   The critical difference is feature selectivity: fixation attention is direction-flat,
   while attention to the opposite-hemifield stimulus is selective for the stimulus motion
   direction (C-017).

3. **Sharpening is width change, not only amplitude change:** A correct Figure 6C model
   output must show a narrower attend-opposite-stimulus tuning curve than the
   attend-fixation curve. A pure vertical rescaling with unchanged width would match the
   Figure 5 multiplicative-scaling pattern, not the Figure 6 feature-based sharpening
   pattern (C-022, C-023).

4. **Preferred direction should not shift:** The attended and fixation curves should peak
   at the same central preferred direction because the recorded model neuron remains fixed
   at theta = 0 and both yoked stimuli have the same swept direction. Feature-based
   attention changes the width of the tuning curve, not the neuron's preferred direction
   (EQ-stim, EQ-attention, C-017).

5. **The narrowing follows from multiplicative feature profiles:** The model multiplies
   stimulus drive by the attention field before normalization, and for Figure 6C the
   attention field is itself tuned in motion direction when the opposite-hemifield stimulus
   is attended. Multiplying direction-selective stimulus drive by direction-selective
   attention makes the effective drive narrower before it is normalized (EQ-5, EQ-6,
   C-005, C-006, C-023).

6. **Panel C differs from contrast-response figures:** Figure 6C should have motion
   direction on the x-axis and no percent-modulation curve. A generated contrast-response
   plot, even with two attention conditions, would be the wrong visual structure for this
   protocol (C-017).
